How to fail your way to a successful project

Why missing the original plan doesn't always mean missing the goal

20 Nov 2023 — Leadership, Project Management, Teams — 6 min read

If you measured my success as an engineering manager by how consistently my projects met their original targets, my track record would be pretty terrible.

I've worked on plenty of projects where the timeline slipped, the scope changed, or the original plan simply stopped making sense. And yet, I consider many of those projects successful. Either I'm remarkably good at rationalizing failure, or there's something worth examining about how we define success.

I think it's the latter. Mostly.

Software development rarely goes exactly as planned. Requirements change, technical challenges emerge, and business priorities shift. Sometimes our original estimates were simply wrong. We can treat every deviation as a failure, or we can recognize that the plan was based on what we understood at the time.

For me, managing a project successfully often comes down to recognizing when something needs to change and figuring out what success should look like now.

Know What You Can Change

When a project starts slipping, one of the first things I want to understand is which parts of the plan are actually negotiable.

The traditional project management triangle gives us three variables to work with: time, scope, and resources. In software engineering, resources are often relatively fixed. Adding engineers might help, but it also introduces coordination and onboarding costs, and those don't disappear just because we're behind schedule.

That usually leaves time and scope as the most practical variables to adjust.

Some projects have deadlines that genuinely can't move. A regulatory requirement or coordinated product launch might need to happen on a specific date, but we may have flexibility in what we deliver. Other projects have requirements that can't reasonably be reduced, but the timeline is negotiable. In those cases, extending the deadline might be the better choice.

Occasionally, both are flexible, which sounds wonderful until nobody can agree on what finished looks like. Without clear expectations, it's difficult to recognize success even when we're making progress.

And then there are projects where both the timeline and scope are fixed. Those are the ones that make me nervous.

If the work can't reasonably be completed with the resources available, something still has to change. Sometimes organizations respond by expecting longer hours or accepting shortcuts in quality. That might get a project across the finish line, but it creates problems we'll eventually have to deal with.

Understanding our constraints gives us a starting point for finding a better outcome.

Negotiate a New Definition of Success

Once I understand which variables can change, the next step is working with stakeholders to redefine what we're trying to accomplish.

That might mean extending a deadline to deliver everything we originally committed to. Or it might mean identifying the most valuable parts of a project and delivering those on time while moving less important work elsewhere.

Either way, the important part is making the decision intentionally.

If we're going to reduce scope, we should understand what we're giving up and why the remaining work is still valuable. If we're extending the timeline, we should have a reasonable understanding of what additional time will accomplish.

I also think it's important to involve stakeholders in these decisions rather than simply announcing that the team needs more time or fewer requirements. The business may have constraints we don't fully understand, just as engineering may be dealing with technical realities that aren't obvious outside the team.

Those conversations can be uncomfortable, especially when we've already made commitments. But I'd rather have a difficult conversation about adjusting expectations than continue working toward a target we no longer believe is realistic.

And when we agree on a new goal, I think it's reasonable to judge the project against that goal.

That's the part that sometimes gets lost. If the business agrees that delivering three features by the deadline is more valuable than delivering five features late, accomplishing those three features should count as a success. We made a deliberate decision about what mattered and delivered on it.

The fact that our original plan looked different doesn't invalidate the result.

Don't Confuse Adaptability With Avoiding Accountability

Of course, there's a risk in this approach. If we redefine success every time a project encounters difficulty, we can make almost anything look successful. That's not particularly useful, and it doesn't build much trust.

I think the distinction comes down to why we're changing expectations and what we learn from the experience.

Sometimes the original plan was reasonable, and circumstances changed in ways we couldn't have predicted. Other times, we underestimated the work, missed important dependencies, or committed to something before we understood the risks.

Both situations may justify changing the plan, but they shouldn't lead to the same conclusions about how the team is performing.

If we're constantly renegotiating deadlines because we keep making the same estimation mistakes, that's something we need to address. The project might still succeed under its revised goals, but we shouldn't ignore the problems that made those revisions necessary.

This is where I think accountability matters most. We can recognize a successful outcome while still being honest about what went wrong along the way.

Those ideas aren't mutually exclusive.

Make Change Something We Can Talk About

One of the challenges with projects going off-plan is the effect they can have on team morale.

When every adjustment feels like a failure, people become reluctant to acknowledge problems. They keep trying to make the original plan work, even when the evidence suggests it's no longer realistic.

I'd rather have a team recognize that something isn't working and bring it up early enough for us to do something about it.

That requires treating changes to a plan as a normal part of software development. Not necessarily something to celebrate, but not something to fear, either.

There's still a difference between healthy adaptation and poor planning. A team experimenting with unfamiliar technology might reasonably change direction several times as it learns. A team repeatedly struggling with familiar work probably needs to examine its process.

The goal isn't to eliminate changes or make every project look successful. It's to build a team that can respond to uncertainty, learn from its mistakes, and make better decisions as new information becomes available.

And when those decisions lead to a successful outcome, I think the team deserves to recognize that success.

Success Isn't Always What We Originally Planned

I've had projects that missed their original targets but delivered exactly what the business needed by the time they finished. I've also had projects where the adjustments were necessary because we made mistakes. Those experiences weren't always pleasant, but they gave us opportunities to improve how we planned and executed future work.

I don't think we should pretend the original targets never existed. They provide useful information about our ability to plan, estimate, and deliver. But they aren't the only way to evaluate whether a project was successful.

As engineering managers, we're responsible for helping teams deliver valuable outcomes, even when the path changes. Sometimes that means protecting the original commitments. Other times, it means recognizing that those commitments no longer represent the best outcome for the business.

If we can have those conversations openly, make intentional tradeoffs, and deliver on the expectations we've agreed to, I think that's worth calling a success.

Even if the original project plan tells a very different story.
