The first 90 days at a new company are a brutal learning curve.

You're learning new systems, names, acronyms, team dynamics, priorities, and unspoken rules, all while trying not to break anything in the process. Even when you're experienced in your role, you're still starting from scratch in a lot of ways.

It's easy to fall into the trap of chasing impact early. You want to prove your worth, make a splash, and show everyone they made the right hire. There's an unspoken pressure to hit the ground running, even when you're still figuring out which direction to run.

I've made that mistake before.

When you're running instead of walking, it's easy to step on toes, especially when you don't yet know who's in the room or how the room even works. You might make some quick improvements, but you're just as likely to overlook important context, solve the wrong problem, or unintentionally undermine relationships you're only beginning to build.

What helped me shift my perspective was realizing something that seems counterintuitive:

**Your job in the first 90 days isn't to deliver. It's to build trust.**

With your manager, your team, your peers, and your cross-functional partners. Everyone you'll depend on to be successful in your new role.

That doesn't mean sitting around waiting to understand everything before taking action. Building trust is hard work. It requires showing up, asking good questions, getting curious about why things work the way they do, admitting when you don't know something, and following through on what you say you'll do.

It also means resisting the temptation to immediately fix everything you notice.

Every organization has decisions that seem strange from the outside. Sometimes they're the result of constraints you haven't discovered yet. Sometimes they're compromises made by people who were solving a different problem. And sometimes they're genuinely opportunities for improvement.

The challenge is knowing the difference.

That's where relationships become so important. The more you understand the people around you, the more context you gain about the work itself. You begin to understand what matters, where the friction exists, and which problems are worth solving first.

And when you've built trust, people are more willing to share what they actually think, challenge your assumptions, and support the changes you eventually make.

None of this means you won't accomplish anything in your first 90 days. You'll still make decisions, solve problems, and contribute to the team. But those outcomes become a natural result of learning and building relationships rather than a desperate attempt to prove you belong.

I think that's the hardest part of starting somewhere new. Accepting that you don't have to demonstrate your full value immediately, even when that little voice in your head insists otherwise.

**And the irony? When you focus on trust, you end up making a bigger impact anyway.**
