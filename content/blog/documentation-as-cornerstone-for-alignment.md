Writing documentation isn't always the most exciting part of software engineering. It takes time, and it's easy to question whether anyone will actually read what we've written. But I've come to appreciate that some of the greatest benefits of documentation happen before anyone else reads it.

When we're discussing a project or making decisions as a team, it's surprisingly easy to feel like everyone understands the same thing. We talk through an approach, answer a few questions, and move forward with what seems like a shared understanding. Putting those ideas into writing forces us to be more deliberate.

## Making Our Thinking Concrete

One of the things I value most about documentation is how it turns abstract conversations into something everyone can examine. Ideas can sound perfectly reasonable when we're discussing them. But once we try to write them down, we sometimes discover that important details are missing, assumptions haven't been questioned, or different people have different interpretations of what was decided.

A written document gives the team a common reference point. Instead of trying to reconcile everyone's memory of a conversation, we can look at the same information and discuss where our understanding differs.

This is particularly useful when making architectural decisions, defining project requirements, or establishing team processes. Small differences in interpretation can lead to considerably different outcomes, especially when people are working independently.

The act of writing also helps us recognize gaps in our own thinking. Something that seemed clear in conversation might be surprisingly difficult to explain on paper. I'd much rather discover that ambiguity while writing a design document than after we've spent two weeks implementing it.

## Keeping That Understanding Over Time

People forget things. Priorities change. Team members move on to different projects, and new people join without the context behind decisions made months or years earlier. Documentation helps preserve that context without relying entirely on someone's memory or availability.

This can be especially valuable in a growing codebase. An architectural decision that made perfect sense when it was made might look questionable years later. Understanding the reasoning behind that decision helps us evaluate whether it still makes sense today.

But documentation doesn't stay useful simply because it exists. Outdated documentation can create just as much confusion as missing documentation. If a document describes how something worked six months ago but no longer reflects reality, it may give people confidence in information they shouldn't trust.

I think it's important to treat documentation as something that evolves alongside the work. That doesn't necessarily mean maintaining every document forever. Some should reflect the current state of a system, while others are useful precisely because they preserve the reasoning behind an earlier decision. Knowing the difference helps us maintain information without creating unnecessary work.

## Understanding Doesn't Guarantee Alignment

Just because we've written something down doesn't mean everyone understands it. And even when everyone understands it, that doesn't necessarily mean they agree.

A document might clearly explain a technical decision while leaving important concerns unresolved. Someone may understand exactly what we're planning to do and still believe it's the wrong approach.

That's not a documentation problem.

Teams bring different experiences, perspectives, and priorities to their work. Sometimes resolving a disagreement requires a conversation rather than another paragraph of explanation. Written and verbal communication complement each other. Documentation gives us something concrete to discuss, while conversation allows us to ask questions, explore disagreements, and work toward a decision.

And sometimes the problem goes beyond communication altogether. We can have excellent documentation of our engineering practices, but that doesn't mean much if we aren't actually following them. A thorough code review guide isn't going to improve code quality if the team isn't taking code reviews seriously.

The document can explain the expectations. The team still has to do the work.

## Documentation Is a Tool, Not a Solution

I don't think writing everything down automatically makes a team more effective. Documentation takes time to create, maintain, and read, and that effort should be justified by the value it provides.

But when teams are making important decisions, working through complicated problems, or trying to maintain a shared understanding over time, I think it's worth investing in. The most useful documentation gives people a common reference point and enough context to understand what was decided and why. Creating it also gives us an opportunity to recognize where our thinking is incomplete and have the conversations needed to address those gaps.

That's where I think much of its value comes from. The process of putting something into words helps us understand the work more clearly, even before anyone else reads what we've written.
