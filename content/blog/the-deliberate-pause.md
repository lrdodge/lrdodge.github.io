---
title: Knowing how to pause is your competitive advantage
date: "2026-07-01"
---

The more I work with AI as an engineering manager, the more I find one skill has become dramatically more valuable.

The deliberate pause.

I know, that sounds backwards.

We're surrounded by tools that let us build faster, write faster, analyze faster, and ship faster. It feels like the people moving the quickest must be winning.

But AI hasn't eliminated the biggest bottleneck.

It's just made it easier to reach it.

You.

The hardest part of most work isn't implementation anymore. It's deciding what deserves to be implemented in the first place.

AI can help you build a feature in half the time, but it can't tell you whether it's the right feature.

It can generate a beautiful report in minutes, but it can't tell you whether anyone should have asked for that report.

I've noticed a subtle trap.

We confuse generation with progress.

One of AI's greatest strengths is making exploration incredibly cheap. That's a good thing. If my team can explore 50 solutions in the time it used to take us to explore five, I'll happily take that trade.

But every solution we generate is another solution competing for our attention.

If 45 of those solutions were never worth considering in the first place, we've also created 45 distractions. We've made the search harder.

That's why I keep coming back to David Marquet's idea of the deliberate pause.

A deliberate pause isn't about slowing down for the sake of slowing down. It's creating a moment to ask better questions before committing your energy.

* What problem are we actually trying to solve?
* Is this the highest-leverage thing we could be working on?
* What would success actually look like?

Five minutes of thinking can save hours of implementation.

Ironically, AI makes that pause even more valuable.

When implementation was expensive, thinking was naturally built into the process. You had time while you were writing code, creating documents, or building presentations.

Now implementation is almost free.

Good judgment isn't.

The engineers who will stand out over the next few years won't necessarily be the ones producing the most output.

They'll be the ones whose output is consistently useful.

That's what builds trust.

That's what makes people the person others want to work with when the work really matters.

AI has made it incredibly easy to create haystacks.

But if your goal is finding needles, creating more hay isn't automatically progress.

The goal was never the haystack.

It was always the needle.
