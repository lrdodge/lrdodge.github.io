---
title: How Halloween teaches us good teamwork
tagline: Collaboration takes both sides of the door
date: "2026-10-29"
---

Trick-or-treating works because a neighborhood agrees to participate.

Some people put on costumes and go door to door. Others turn on their porch lights and get a bowl of candy ready. Each side puts in effort, and together they create something neither could make happen alone.

I’ve been thinking about how much workplace collaboration depends on that same exchange. We ask people to speak up, seek advice, and reach across teams. But those actions only work when someone on the other side is ready to welcome them.

## You have to be willing to knock

If you want candy on Halloween, you have to leave the house. You have to walk up to a door, knock, and ask.

Asking for help at work can feel surprisingly similar.

You might need a colleague’s perspective, an introduction, or someone to explain a process you don’t understand. Before you get any of those things, you have to make your need visible.

That can be uncomfortable. Asking means admitting you don’t have every answer. It means interrupting someone, risking a rejection, or wondering whether you should have figured it out yourself.

It’s easy to let that discomfort make the decision for you. You keep working alone, spend another afternoon trying to solve the same problem, or wait for someone to notice that you’re stuck.

Sometimes independent effort is exactly what a situation needs. But when another person’s knowledge would help you move forward, staying silent has a cost too.

A useful request doesn’t need to be elaborate. Explain what you’re trying to accomplish, what you’ve already tried, and where you could use help. Give the other person a clear way to contribute.

You don’t have to feel completely comfortable before you knock.

## People need a reason to approach your door

The other half of the exchange is just as important.

On Halloween, a dark porch sends a clear signal. Most people won’t walk up to a house with the lights off and no sign that anyone is home.

At work, our signals can be harder to recognize—especially our own.

We may say, “My door is always open,” while reacting to questions with impatience. We may encourage people to ask for help, then make them feel embarrassed for needing it. We may invite collaboration but rarely make time for the conversations it requires.

Over time, people learn which signal to trust.

Being approachable takes more than announcing your availability. It shows up in how you respond when someone actually approaches you.

Do you listen before assuming they should know the answer? Can you explain something without making the person feel small? If you’re busy, do you offer a realistic time to reconnect?

You don’t need to be available every minute. A clear, considerate boundary can make asking easier: “I can’t give this my attention right now, but I have fifteen minutes tomorrow morning.”

That gives someone a path forward—and a reason to come back.

## Be ready when someone shows up

Turning on the porch light is an invitation. Having candy ready is how you follow through.

The workplace equivalent is giving a request some genuine attention. That might mean sharing your experience, asking a question that helps someone think, or connecting them with the right person.

You won’t always have the answer. You can still help someone find their next step.

What matters is whether the experience reinforces the invitation. When someone takes the risk of asking, your response helps determine whether they’ll do it again.

## Make the next conversation easier

Most of us spend time on both sides of the door. We need help in one area and have something to offer in another.

That gives us two ways to improve collaboration: make a clear request when we need support, and respond thoughtfully when someone asks us.

Neither guarantees a perfect exchange. But each makes the next conversation a little easier.

A neighborhood comes alive on Halloween through lots of small acts of participation. A team builds trust much the same way: one person knocks, another welcomes them, and both have a reason to keep showing up.
